Solutions: Working Together

Introduction

AI data centers don't have to be shut down, because they provide real benefits. But they do need to be planned and managed carefully. Here's what local residents and local government can each do.

For local residents

Get involved early. Go to public hearings and planning board meetings, and ask questions before a data center is approved: How much water and electricity will it use, and where will that come from? Residents have real influence. More than 140 local groups have blocked or delayed over $60 billion in data center projects in about a year (Yale School of the Environment, 2026). Working with companies and officials can turn disagreement into a shared plan.

For local government

Bring every party to the table (companies, utilities, and residents) to evaluate resource needs, environmental effects, and long-term sustainability before approving a project. Officials can require water-saving air cooling, especially in high-stress areas (Ginzburg-Ganz et al., 2026, p. 20), steer facilities away from water-stressed regions (Yale School of the Environment, 2026), and require efficient, automated cooling systems (Ginzburg-Ganz et al., 2026, p. 23).

Better cooling, less water

Option 1

Air cooling

Fans and vents blow air over the servers to carry heat away instead of using water, leaving more water for the community (Ginzburg-Ganz et al., 2026, p. 20).

Option 2

Smart locations

Building near plentiful water or in colder regions helps, because it matters greatly whether a facility is in a water-stressed region (Yale School of the Environment, 2026).

Option 3

Efficient systems

Like a smart thermostat, these systems sense heat and cool only as much as the servers' workload needs, saving water and energy (Ginzburg-Ganz et al., 2026, p. 23).
